Slime Transit

Scorecard

Each network is measured the same way. Slime results are the average of several runs, with the range in brackets.

At about the same length as today's rail, the slime reaches every population centre and joins every pair of them, with similar directness and similar resilience to a broken link. Today's rail puts more residents near a line: it runs through the densest corridors instead of reaching every outlying town.

How it works

Food. The 2020 census places 1,678 neighbourhoods (tracts) in this window. Grouping them by population gives 30 centres. Each becomes a food source, stronger where more people live.

Water. The Bay and the ocean are walls. The slime may cross only where a bridge or tunnel crosses today: the Golden Gate, the Bay Bridge and Transbay Tube, the San Mateo, Dumbarton and Richmond–San Rafael bridges.

Slime. About 4,900 agents follow Jeff Jones's model of Physarum polycephalum: sniff the trail ahead, turn towards the strongest, leave more behind. After 4,000 steps the busiest trails are the network. A second model, Tero's flow model from the famous Tokyo rail experiment, grows tubes that thicken with the flow they carry.

Scoring. Every network is thinned to its centre lines and measured: length, how many centres and residents are within 2 km, how directly it joins centres, and how much of it could fail without cutting any centre off.

What it leaves out

The slime sees flat land and water, nothing else. It runs straight over the East Bay hills and the Santa Cruz mountains, ignores what tunnels cost, and knows nothing of ridership, existing roads or buses and ferries. The comparison counts rail only (BART, Caltrain, VTA, Muni Metro, SMART, ACE and the Capitol Corridor). Treat it as a way to see the shape of the region's demand, not as a transit plan.
